Ask an organisation who owns the system prompt and watch the room go quiet.

It's a fair question. A system prompt encodes what the product does, what it refuses, how it talks, what it cites, and what happens when it isn't sure. That's product policy, legal exposure, brand voice, and risk posture in one text file. In most teams it's owned by whoever wrote it during the pilot.

On the systems I've delivered, ownership got split on purpose, the way it would be for any other artefact that touches multiple functions.

Engineering owns the mechanics: structure, tool descriptions, output schemas, the parts that make the system work.
Product owns the behaviour: what it does, what it declines, the tone.
Risk or compliance owns the boundaries: what it may never assert, what it must always disclose, what needs a human.
One named person owns the whole, and any change needs the relevant owner's review before it runs through the harness.

That sounds heavy. In practice it's a pull request template and a review rule. What it prevents is the Friday-afternoon prompt edit that changes what the system promises customers.

Who owns yours?
