The assessment readouts that changed anything fit on one page.

Not because the work was small. Thirty-two gaps and a dependency map don't fit on a page. But the decision does: here is what we found, here is what it costs to leave alone, here is what we recommend, here is who owns each part, here is what we need from you.

I learned this the hard way, presenting sixty-slide decks to executives who had twenty minutes and a decision to make. The deck was thorough. The decision didn't happen, because the deck never quite said what it was.

Now the page comes first and the deck is the appendix. Writing the page is the actual work. It forces you to know what you're recommending, what you're not sure about, and what you're asking for. If it won't fit, the thinking isn't done.

The same rule applies to architecture decisions, incident reviews, and pilot exit criteria. One page, a recommendation, a name, a date.

The deck is for the people who want the evidence. The page is for the person who has to decide.

When did you last write the page before the deck?
