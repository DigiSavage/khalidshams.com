Estimates get rounded to what the room wants to hear.

I've watched it happen in solution assessments, in migration plans, in AI pilots. The honest answer is "somewhere between four and nine months depending on three things we don't know yet." What gets written down is "six months," because that fits the slide and the fiscal year.

Then month seven arrives, and the person who said six is explaining. The three unknowns were real. They just weren't on the slide.

I've stopped giving point estimates for anything that has real uncertainty in it. What I give instead is a range, the assumptions that put you at each end, and what it would cost to resolve the biggest unknown early.

"Four months if the source system's data quality is what the team believes. Nine if it's what I've seen in similar estates. A two-week profiling exercise would tell us which."

It's less satisfying in the meeting. It's far better in month seven, because the conversation is about which assumption broke, not about who was wrong.

The range is the honest number. The point estimate is a wish.

When did a point estimate last cost you credibility?
